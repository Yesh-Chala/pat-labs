Our why

Why we exist

As the world’s largest companies race to make technology more capable, we study what that technology is doing to the people at the end of the line, so that progress can be steered toward a more human-centric world.

Every day, we build more capable systems, find new uses for them, and bring them into more parts of our lives. Along the way, the ways we think, work, connect, make decisions and understand one another are changing too. Some of these changes are visible. Others take shape slowly, through the habits we form and the expectations we come to live with.

We work under the principle that each major technological update sends out a ripple. Ripples meet other ripples, and while some fade out, others build into waves that change how people behave, feel, relate to each other and make meaning of their lives.

Frontier technology labs bring rigorous research to bear on the technology itself. We bring that same rigor to the human counterpart, studying how advanced technologies are reflected on, used and lived with, and what that means for the kind of world we are building.

We also build the tools to do this work. Along with our studies, we develop testbeds, metrics and evaluation tools that help us understand human behavior around technology and measure it better.

We think these changes deserve to be studied, understood and talked about.

Emotional Imbalances in Everyday Life

Awareness, cognitive dissonance and everyday digital self-regulation among college students in India

We interviewed 14 undergraduate students from tier 2 and tier 3 cities in India about their everyday phone use. Most were already aware that their use had costs, and most had tried to reduce it. This report describes the motivation for the study, the participants and method, the findings, and their implications for the design of digital wellbeing tools.

01

Introduction

Digital self-control tools are now widely available. Google launched Digital Wellbeing and Apple launched Screen Time in 2018, and apps like Forest, BlockSite and Opal are among the most popular. Most of these tools are built on one of two ideas: show people how much they use their phone, or block the apps they want to use less.

Evidence on their effectiveness is mixed. One study of popular smartphone interventions found that users kept to only around 2% of timer-based interventions (Monge Roffarello and De Russis, 2019). A later meta-analysis by the same authors found at most a short-term effect on time spent on the device, with no long-term effect shown (Monge Roffarello and De Russis, 2022).

This study examines a situation in which awareness of overuse does not appear to be sufficient, and existing tools offer limited help. We interviewed college students about how they experience the gap between how they intend to use their phones and how they actually use them. The study addressed three research questions:

  • How do Indian college students describe their everyday relationship with their phones and their digital wellbeing?
  • How do they experience the gap between intended and actual use, how does that gap look across three kinds of awareness, and how does it turn into what we call Digital Cognitive Dissonance?
  • How do digital self-control tools fit into their attempts to regulate their use, and what does that tell us about better interventions?

02

Background

India has a large, young and highly connected population. Prior research indicates the scale of use:

  • Over 750 million mobile internet users in 2025 (Reuters, 2026).
  • Adolescents and young adults report 4.5 to 6 hours of non-educational screen time a day (Gangadharan et al., 2022).
  • In a study of undergraduate medical students in Telangana, 83.2% spent more than 4 hours a day on their phones, and 47% spent more than 6 (Jahagirdar et al., 2021).
  • Meta-analytic estimates put smartphone and internet addiction among Indian youth at 24% to 44% (Davey and Davey, 2014; Das et al., 2024).

The Economic Survey 2025–26 also named compulsive digital engagement among young people as an emerging social, psychological and economic concern (Government of India).

Most public and policy responses treat this as overuse, something to be measured and reduced. For students, however, the phone is also where they study, complete coursework and stay in touch, which makes reduction difficult to achieve cleanly. Awareness alone also appears to have limited effect. A study in Maharashtra found that knowing about and liking digital wellbeing features was not associated with lower screen time or lower addiction scores (Ambike et al., 2023).

Earlier work also shows that when people’s attempts to control their use fail, they can be left with guilt, shame and frustration (Carver and Scheier, 1990; Schønning et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2019). Little research has examined how this is experienced by users themselves, particularly in India, and this study addresses that gap.

03

Terminology

TermWhat we mean by it
Digital Cognitive DissonanceThe discomfort people feel when how they think they should use their phone does not match how they actually use it. We extend cognitive dissonance theory (Harmon-Jones and Mills, 2019) to everyday digital use, where the use people value and the use they want to change sit on the same device, and often in the same app.
Behavioural awarenessHow well a person recognises their own patterns: which apps, how much time, and in what situations. It maps to self-monitoring in Bandura’s theory of self-regulation (Bandura, 1991).
Consequential awarenessHow well a person connects their use to its effects on attention, mood, sleep, relationships, productivity and sense of self. It maps to Bandura’s judgment process.
Regulatory awarenessHow well a person understands what they do about their use, which tools or strategies they try, and why those attempts work or fail. It is built from Bandura’s self-reaction process.
Invisible standardThe shared sense of good and bad phone use that participants judged themselves against, without being able to say where it came from.
DSCTDigital self-control tool. App timers, screen time limits, blockers, focus modes and similar features.

The three kinds of awareness do not have to move together. Someone can know exactly how much they use their phone and still not connect that use to the problems they are having.

04

Method and participants

Participants. 16 people enrolled. Two left early and we excluded their partial data, which left 14 participants: 7 women and 7 men, all undergraduate students between 18 and 25. Twelve were 18 to 20 and two were 21 to 25. They came from tier 2 and tier 3 cities, nine in Andhra Pradesh and five in Nagpur, Maharashtra, and most described where they live as rural or semi-urban.

Recruitment. We used convenience sampling through our personal and community networks. The call for participation went out on WhatsApp statuses and in community groups, and described the study as a one-hour interview about everyday digital habits. Interested participants completed a short form and booked a slot through a shared Google Calendar link.

Consent. The study did not go through a formal Institutional Review Board or ethics committee. At the start of each session the interviewer explained what the study was for and went through consent. Participants were told they could skip any question and withdraw at any point, and interviews were recorded only with their consent.

Interviews. The interviews were semi-structured and happened either over Zoom or in person with an audio recording. Each one opened with a question about the person’s daily digital habits, and then followed a flexible guide built around awareness, cognitive dissonance and everyday habits. We adapted the guide from earlier qualitative work on how people talk about their own device use (Alharahsheh et al., 2021; Harkin and Kuss, 2021; Lanette et al., 2018).

Language. Nine participants chose to speak in Telugu, often mixed with English. We transcribed these interviews word for word with Gemini, keeping the language mixing, and a native Telugu speaker on the team checked every transcript against the audio. The English translations were also made with Gemini’s help, and an external reviewer fluent in both languages checked them against the originals. We did not compute a formal translation quality score.

Analysis. Two researchers coded all 14 interviews independently, with codes for the three kinds of awareness, for cognitive dissonance, and for everyday habits as context. Disagreements were discussed until consensus was reached, which produced three construct-level dossiers. We then rebuilt each participant’s day in order, starting from when they woke up and using only what they reported, and mapped awareness and dissonance onto that sequence. Finally, we re-examined all material coded as regulatory awareness to identify patterns in how people used, overrode, dropped or reinstated self-control tools.

05

Pre-interview survey

The form participants completed before the interview also included a set of agree and disagree statements about their phone use. Because these responses come from 14 people, we treat them as a description of this group only. They nonetheless provide useful context for the interview findings.

  • 12 of 14 agreed their phone use has negative effects on their wellbeing, and 10 agreed that reducing it would help.
  • 9 had tried to reduce their use and found it difficult, and 9 said they spend more time on their phone than they intend to.
  • 9 also agreed their phone is essential to them, which partly explains why reduction is difficult.
  • Responses were divided on phone management tools: 8 said they would try one and 5 said they would not.

When asked why they use their phones, learning and managing tasks were the reasons most participants agreed with, 10 of 14 each. Escape and fear of missing out were the reasons they agreed with least. These responses should be read alongside the interviews, since productive use was also the kind of use participants felt was expected of them, and some answers may reflect the same standard.

06 · Findings

Cognitive dissonance

Discomfort arising from a mismatch between intended and actual phone use was evident across the interviews. It appeared in three forms.

Automatic use. Many participants described reaching for their phone or opening an app without deciding to, and then catching themselves partway through and questioning it. Instagram Reels and WhatsApp statuses came up the most.

Tools that did not hold. Several participants had used self-control tools. They described setting limits and then removing them, or finding ways around them.

Guilt, regret and frustration. When their use did not match what they had intended, participants described guilt, regret, frustration and a feeling that the time had been wasted.

Participants also pointed to the design of the apps themselves. They described thumbnails that pulled them into longer videos, recommendations that seemed to know what they liked, and feeds that filled up with more of whatever they had just watched. They also described the justifications they used to continue, like deciding the content was useful enough to justify more time, so the choice to keep using was made again and again in the moment.

07 · Findings

Behavioural, consequential and regulatory awareness

We examined awareness across three modes, and participants differed considerably between them.

Behavioural awareness was the clearest. Participants knew which apps pulled them in and when. Many said they use social media most at night. Instagram and YouTube Shorts came up during boredom or commutes, WhatsApp in idle moments, and gaming sometimes late at night. Some were surprised by their own screen time.

Consequential awareness was the weakest. Participants named immediate effects, mainly eyesight problems and headaches, losing track of time, and mood shifts that followed the content they watched. One described sad reels making her feel progressively sadder, and another said comedy reels helped him forget what was bothering him. They rarely connected their use to longer-term effects on their attention, mood or goals.

Regulatory awareness was active, but it did not last. Participants had tried a wide range of strategies, from native timers and deleting apps to greyscale screens and switching off YouTube watch history. P1 set and removed app timers several times, and P7 laughed about breaking their own 15-minute Instagram limit every day. A few strategies were more deliberate: P8 set a goal before opening an app and went only to the page they needed, and P1 routed notifications through email to give themselves time to decide whether to open the app. Participants knew which strategies were available to them, and the gap lay in sustaining them.

08 · Contribution

The invisible standard

This was the most prevalent pattern across the interviews. Participants had a clear sense of good and bad phone use. Studying and other productive work counted as good, and most other things counted as bad, whatever the person’s own reason for doing them. Someone who used Instagram to relax, or to build a following, still saw that use as negative.

When we asked where this sense of good and bad came from, no one could name a source. They did not point to their family, their school or college, or any other authority.

This resembles what Cialdini and colleagues call an injunctive norm, a shared sense of what one ought to do that exists apart from a person’s own goals (Cialdini et al., 1990). It then gets taken in as a personal standard for judging oneself, which is how Bandura describes socially learned standards (Bandura, 1991). A norm absorbed from a shared environment is experienced as common knowledge, which would explain why participants held it so strongly and still could not trace it.

The standard was also applied unevenly. Two participants who shared the goal of improving their programming skills judged Instagram in opposite ways: one saw it as a distraction, and the other used it to find learning resources and studied through it. Participants applied the standard to other people too, and several described others’ phone use as excessive without thinking about why those people were using their phones.

Our analysis notes also suggest a difference by place. Participants from rural areas tended to hold the standard more strictly, and some counted even watching a movie as wasted time. Participants from Nagpur were more relaxed about it, although they still had a point past which their use felt wrong. Because this observation rests on a small number of interviews, we treat it as a question for future work.

09 · Findings

Guilt and its management

Guilt was the emotion that came up most when participants’ use did not meet the standard. Frustration, resentment, regret and shame came up too. When their use did not feel productive, participants felt they had not made any progress and that the time had been wasted. P5 described watching reels as exams got closer and then feeling the time was lost, and another participant remembered spending nearly two hours on Instagram without knowing why. For some, this turned into shame about feeling they had lost control.

Most of the strategies participants used to manage this guilt aimed at relieving the feeling. Some switched to a productive activity immediately afterwards to offset it. Some uninstalled the app they blamed. Others justified their use before starting, for example by opening their saved reels or a specific tutorial account, and then drifted into unrelated content.

Both approaches ran into the same problem. The apps participants used for studying were the same apps they drifted on, so there was no clean line between the use they wanted to keep and the use they wanted to drop. Deleting or restricting an app took away both, and losing the useful part was one of the reasons these measures did not last.

Across these accounts, four recurring patterns accounted for most of the failures.

  • Unrealistic limits. Limits were often set too low for apps people needed for communication and coursework. A 30-minute cap on WhatsApp could not survive an ordinary day. When a real need came up the limit was removed, and after removing it once, removing it again got easier.
  • Content interruption. Timers often ran out partway through a reel or a video. Participants overrode the timer to finish what they were watching, and then kept scrolling.
  • Justified override. Participants lifted restrictions by pointing to a possible future need, like an emergency or a productive task. Some of these needs were real, but often the need was hypothetical, and it gave them permission to remove a barrier they already wanted gone.
  • Delete and reinstall. This was the most revealing pattern. Deleting an app, usually Instagram, brought immediate relief and a feeling of being back in control, and once the relief wore off the app came back. Participants said the relief got smaller each time, so the act carried on even after it had stopped giving them much.

11 · Exploratory

Rubric-based awareness scores

Read these as exploratory. We applied a scoring rubric to the transcripts with the help of an AI model, and no second human coder has checked the scores, so we do not have inter-rater reliability for them. For that reason we left them out of the paper. We include them here because they offer an approximate picture of the same pattern we saw in the interviews.

Each transcript was assigned four scores on a scale of 0 to 5. Three of them were for the three kinds of awareness. The fourth, which we called the dichotomy score, was for how strongly the person expressed a gap between how they think they should use their phone and how they do. To score 2 or more on dichotomy, a participant had to state a view about how phones should be used and also describe behaviour that did not match it. A 5 meant the mismatch persistently interfered with daily life.

The scores follow the same ordering observed in the interviews. Behavioural awareness was the highest and the most consistent, with every participant at 3 or 4 (mean 3.71). Consequential awareness was the lowest and the most spread out (mean 3.14), with four participants at 2. Regulatory awareness sat in between (mean 3.21), with nine of the 14 at 3. On dichotomy, 10 of 14 scored 4 or 5 (mean 3.71).

With 14 people, the correlations between the awareness scores and the dichotomy score were small and not statistically significant, so we do not draw conclusions from them.

12 · Contribution

The Digital Normative Dissociation Framework

This framework is provisional. It comes from 14 interviews and our own interpretation of them, and we have not tested it. In the paper we present its core as Digital Cognitive Dissonance. We are sharing the fuller version here because it is the clearest way we have found to describe what participants went through, and we welcome critique of it.

The framework starts from the invisible standard. In summary, participants judged themselves against a standard they could not source, for behaviour they did not consciously choose, that they could only see in hindsight. We describe this as a form of double blindness, because people cannot see where the standard comes from, and they also cannot see the behaviour while it is happening.

Contributions

  • Digital Cognitive Dissonance

    An extension of cognitive dissonance theory to everyday phone use. Dissonance is typically reduced by changing the behaviour, changing the belief, or explaining the gap away. When the use people value and the use they want to change share the same device, and often the same app, each of these routes is difficult to sustain.

  • The invisible standard

    Evidence that students judge their phone use against a productivity-based norm they hold strongly but cannot trace, and that this judgment is where a lot of their discomfort comes from.

  • Three kinds of awareness

    A layered way of looking at awareness, built from Bandura’s theory of self-regulation. It shows how people can know their patterns well and still connect them only weakly to their consequences.

  • How self-control tools play out in real days

    An account of how timers, limits, notification controls and deletion are used, overridden, dropped and reinstated, including the four failure patterns and the one strategy that held.

  • The Digital Normative Dissociation Framework

    A provisional model of the guilt and control cycle that ties these findings together.

14

Design implications

The findings point to three directions for digital wellbeing.

The standard. Since participants judged themselves against a shared norm instead of their own goals, interventions should not swap in another outside standard. A more promising approach may be to make the inherited standard visible as a norm, and to bring people’s own goals into how they judge their use. This probably matters most in places where phone habits are rarely talked about.

The guilt. A lot of the discomfort we saw seemed to come from the idea that phones are only acceptable for productive use, more than from actual harm. Approaches that treat phone use as an ordinary part of daily life could help. Screen-time dashboards and usage reports should also be checked, so they do not add guilt without changing anything.

The device. Useful use and drifting use will keep sharing the same screen, so separating them by blocking or removing apps is unlikely to hold. A more suitable target is helping people distinguish, in the moment, the use they came for and the drift they fall into.

These directions involve several stakeholders:

  • Designers: short reflective moments at the points where use turns automatic, like a prompt when an app is opened out of habit, or a reminder of the goal during a session.
  • Platforms: taking on some of the responsibility for regulation, as several participants asked for. The same curation and interface choices that drive automatic use could be adjusted to interrupt it, for example by starting a session with what the person came to do.
  • Policymakers: going beyond screen-time disclosures, which assume that informing people is enough, and setting expectations for product design.
  • Educators and public conversation: talking about phone use as an ordinary necessity, and questioning the standards people inherit.

Participants also proposed their own solutions: a phone that reminds them of their goals every couple of hours, apps that stop working completely once a limit is reached, and phone companies building in better restrictions.

Limitations and future work

  • Small, similar sample. 14 undergraduates from tier 2 and tier 3 cities, recruited through convenience sampling. The findings are not meant to be representative or to generalise.
  • One point in time. The study is cross-sectional, so we cannot say how habits, awareness and self-regulation change over time.
  • Several languages. We were careful with transcription and translation, but differences in language, dialect and expression may have shaped how we read some responses.
  • Self-report. Everything comes from what participants told us, which can be shaped by what they think sounds acceptable.
  • Researcher proximity. We share some demographic background with participants and are familiar with the habits we were studying, which may have shaped the interviews.
  • Exploratory scores and framework. The awareness scores have no inter-rater reliability, and the framework is untested.

Future work should draw on larger and more varied samples, and longitudinal studies that follow how habits, awareness and self-regulation develop. Digital Cognitive Dissonance also needs to be studied more directly, especially its emotional, behavioural and social effects on young people in India. One of our current directions is a measure of the guilt, regret and frustration people link to their phone use.

A period-weighted, compositional index of a day of smartphone use, and a pilot of the Build Your Phone Day instrument

This report documents the instrument, its 79-block vocabulary, the scoring specification and the normative model behind its weights, and describes the first public deployment, which we treat as a pilot of the measure. 01

Introduction

Screen time is the default summary of smartphone use in research and in consumer self-tracking tools. It is easy to collect and easy to compare, and it compresses very different activities into a single duration. Consider two people who each log five hours. One coordinated work during the day, called family in the evening and read before bed. The other woke up into an hour of reels, drifted through the afternoon and fell asleep while scrolling at 2 a.m. Screen time gives both days the same number, although they differ in almost every respect that matters to the person living them.

Our earlier study, Emotional Imbalances in Everyday Life, pointed to a second problem. Participants already knew their screen time totals and their most-used apps, and that knowledge rarely changed what they did. When they judged their own use, they asked other questions: whether it was intentional or automatic, productive or draining, and whether it suited the time of day. The same scrolling could feel harmless in the evening and regrettable straight after waking or late at night.

The Phone Day Score (PDS) is built on those dimensions. Participants use Build Your Phone Day to rebuild a day of phone use from a fixed set of activity blocks across four periods of the day, and the score is computed from what they placed, when they placed it and in what order. The scoring specification is implemented and deployed. Its coefficients were set by hand from the literature and our qualitative work, the attributes of each block were assigned by us, and the deployment data is descriptive. We publish the specification at this stage so that its assumptions can be criticised and so that later validation has a fixed target.

This report addresses three questions:

  • Can the composition, intentionality, timing and order of a day of phone use be described with a fixed vocabulary of activity blocks?
  • What normative model does the score encode, and how is that model implemented?
  • What does a first public deployment, treated as a pilot, show about the feasibility of the instrument and the behaviour of the specification?

02

Background

Why we wanted a different measure. Screen time, notification counts and the number of installed apps were adequate summaries of phone use while that use was narrow and broadly similar from one person to the next. They have become less informative as education, employment, healthcare and social belonging have come to depend on digital access, so that no two people now have the same experience when they are online. A decade or more ago, several hours of use outside work or school could reasonably be read as a warning sign because such levels were uncommon, whereas the same figure now describes very different days. An hour of tutorial videos and an hour of habitual scrolling register identically, and an identical total can reflect professional necessity, intentional leisure or compulsive escape. A social media content promoter may log sixteen hours of screen time because that is their job. There is also no agreed standard for how many hours of a given activity are healthy or harmful, and in its absence people rely on informal advice and on a private sense of good and bad use.

What a duration standard does. We see two consequences of treating total time as the standard. The first is that it sets expectations of healthy use that are often unrealistic, so that people swing between extreme measures, such as deleting apps or attempting long breaks, and abandoning the effort altogether. The second is that it points the available tools at access rather than at behaviour. A limit or a blocker can close an app for a few hours without helping a person understand why they reached for the phone, or change the habit behind it. Duration remains a reasonable starting point, since awareness of how much time goes to a device is where most people begin. The PDS is our attempt to describe what that time is made of, so that the same number of hours can be read differently for different people and different days, without condensing use that is dynamic, subjective and dependent on context into a single objective figure.

Screen time as a construct. Screen time began as a measure of television exposure and carried over to smartphones largely because duration was what devices could report. Kaye and colleagues describe the resulting literature as one of “conceptual and methodological mayhem”, in which studies operationalise use inconsistently and aggregate across unrelated activities (Kaye et al., 2020). Large analyses of adolescent wellbeing find that associations between digital technology use and negative outcomes are small and sensitive to analytic choices (Orben and Przybylski, 2019), and self-reported use correlates only moderately with logged use (Parry et al., 2021).

Beyond duration. Researchers have often separated active from passive use, although a recent meta-analysis found that this distinction alone is weakly related to most wellbeing outcomes (Godard and Holtzman, 2024). Other work shows that people distinguish meaningful sessions from habitual sessions that fill time (Lukoff et al., 2018), and research on problematic use increasingly emphasises habit and loss of control (van Deursen et al., 2015). Together these strands support a multidimensional description of use, and they also warn against treating any single dimension as good or bad in itself.

Time of day. When use happens carries information that total duration discards. Bedtime phone use is associated with poorer sleep quality and more fatigue, although most of this evidence is observational (Exelmans and Van den Bulck, 2016). Evidence for strong downstream effects of the first phone action after waking is thinner. The PDS gives morning behaviour substantial weight because waking-time automaticity was especially salient in our qualitative study, and we treat that choice as a design hypothesis.

The gap. Operating system dashboards report totals and per-app breakdowns, and digital self-control tools act on access through limits, locks and friction, with modest short-term effects (Monge Roffarello and De Russis, 2023). None of these jointly represent the composition of use, its intentionality, its time of day and its order. Passive sensing can capture timing and some composition, but it requires logging infrastructure, carries privacy costs and cannot observe intentionality. Structured reconstruction is inexpensive and can represent these internal dimensions, while remaining exposed to recall error, self-presentation and the limits of a fixed vocabulary.

04

Qualitative grounding

Every scored measure of behaviour encodes a position on what counts as better and worse, and we state ours openly so that it can be examined. Much of its shape comes from Emotional Imbalances in Everyday Life, a study that combined a pre-interview survey, day reconstruction and semi-structured interviews with 14 college students aged 18 to 25 from tier 2 and tier 3 cities in India. Three findings shaped the index.

  1. A standard nobody could source

    Participants held a confident sense of good use (productivity, learning, deliberate communication) and bad use (scrolling, passive entertainment), and none could say where it came from. A scored measure would not impose a foreign value system, since participants already scored themselves. It would need to make its standard explicit and open to inspection.

  2. A day with a consistent arc

    Mornings began with the alarm, notifications and messages, often sliding into a scroll before getting up. Midday use was functional and communicative. Evenings were for consumption and largely free of guilt. Night was where control failed. Guilt depended on the time of day, so a measure that weights use uniformly across the day would miss this structure.

  3. Automatic use, seen afterwards

    The behaviour participants regretted most was automatic. They described finding themselves mid-scroll with no decision they could recall, and they could observe these sessions only in retrospect. This is why intentionality is a first-class attribute, and why the instrument asks people to reconstruct the day in order.

The normative model. Use that is intentional, active or restorative is rewarded, most strongly at the anchoring points of the day, morning and night. Use that is avoidant, depleting or easily replaceable is penalised, most strongly at those same points. Consumption counts mildly against the score in the morning and at midday and mildly for it in the evening, where bounded entertainment is an ordinary use of leisure. Communication is rewarded only in the evening, when it tends to be social. These are choices, and a different research team could defend different weights. The sensitivity analysis in the validation plan is designed to show how much the behaviour of the score depends on them.

05

The instrument

Build Your Phone Day is a web instrument in which a participant rebuilds a day of phone use by selecting activity blocks. The four period palettes appear together on one screen, and each block is added to the tower when tapped. The tower stays hidden until the participant chooses to reveal it, and a strip below the palettes shows the three most recent additions, each with an undo control that also removes every block placed after it. This matches the working paper, which describes showing only the last three blocks placed as a way of keeping the task closer to reconstruction than to self-presentation. The effect of this design on recall and impression management has not been tested.

Vocabulary. The vocabulary has 79 labelled blocks. “Watch something” appears in both the evening and the night palettes, so the four palettes hold 80 placements: 17 in the early morning, 22 at mid-morning and afternoon, 24 in the evening and 17 at night. The blocks cover professional activity, communication, passing time, hobbies, self-care, logistics and compulsive patterns, from “turn off alarm” and “check weather” to “keep scrolling” and “snooze & go back”. A fixed vocabulary trades expressiveness for comparability, since every day is composed from the same units.

Two layers of annotation. Each block has an identifier, a display label and one of six categories. Each block also has four behavioural attributes, intentionality, mode, effect and replaceability, which were assigned by us with reference to the literature and the qualitative study. Ambiguous activities, such as checking email or playing a game, were assigned “mixed” where neither source supported a firm assignment.

What is recorded. Each submission stores a timestamp and, for every block, its position in the tower, its identifier, label, category and period. No names, contact details or demographic fields are collected. The instrument records order, which duration measures and most surveys discard. It does not record how long any activity lasted.

06

Scoring specification

This section documents the scoring specification as described in the working paper and the technical notes on the implementation. The pipeline is deterministic:

  1. Each block in the tower is looked up by its label and given its four attributes.
  2. For each period that has blocks, the share of blocks with each property is computed.
  3. Each period receives a score between 0 and 100 from its own weighted model.
  4. Period scores are combined with fixed period weights.
  5. A first-action term is added from the category of the first block.
  6. Sequence penalties are subtracted, and the result is clamped to the range 0 to 100.
  7. Interpretive outputs (a band, tags, one piece of advice and a short narrative) are produced, and none of them feed back into the score.

Period features

For a property X and a set of blocks S, the feature is a simple proportion, defined as 0 when S is empty.

Pct_S(X) = |{ b in S : b has X }| / |S|

For each period with blocks, the model uses the proportions of intentional, reactive, active, passive, restorative and depleting blocks, the proportion with high replaceability, and the proportion in each of the six categories.

I is intentional, A active, R restorative, P planning, S self-care, U utility, Com communication, Re reactive, Pa passive, Av avoidance, H high replaceability, D depleting and C consumption, each as a proportion of the blocks in that period.

Reasons for the main choices. Intentionality carries its largest weight in the early morning, where automatic pickup was most salient in our interviews. Restorative weight rises toward night, where recovery is the main legitimate function of pre-sleep activity, and depleting weight peaks there for the same reason. Consumption changes sign in the evening, because a measure that punishes all consumption at all times would reproduce the guilt-generating standard our participants already described. Communication appears only in the evening model because evening communication in our data was mostly social, while daytime communication was mostly obligatory. Reactive and passive terms drop out of the evening and night models because the positive terms and the depleting and avoidance penalties already carry the relevant contrast there, and counting the same behaviour under two negative terms made night scores excessively punitive in early testing.

Aggregation across the day

W = { early morning 0.30, mid-morning 0.25, evening 0.20, night 0.15 }

PeriodScore = Σ_present W_p × Score_p / Σ_present W_p
Overall     = Clamp( 0.9 × PeriodScore + 0.1 × FirstActionScore − Penalties )

The declining weights encode the proposal that earlier periods matter more to the shape of the day, and they are a central target for sensitivity testing. When a tower has no blocks in a period, the weights are renormalised over the periods that are present, so an unreported period is treated as unobserved and does not lower the score. The four weights sum to 0.90, so when all four periods are present the period contribution, 0.9 × PeriodScore, equals the weighted sum of the four period scores.

Pilot deployment

Build Your Phone Day was deployed publicly between 21 March and 14 June 2026 and recorded 31 submitted sessions. Of these, 24 were submitted on 21 March, the day the tool was shared, and the remaining 7 arrived over the following twelve weeks. We treat this first data collection as a pilot of the measure. It can show whether people complete the task, which parts of the vocabulary they use and whether the resulting records are interpretable. It cannot validate the score, establish population distributions or support comparisons between groups.

Cleaning. We removed 5 sessions with a single block, which appeared abandoned or exploratory, and 1 session of 80 blocks that selected every placement in all four palettes. That left 25 towers containing 327 blocks. The analytic unit is a submitted tower, and several towers may come from the same person.

08

Pilot results

Composition of the day

Consumption was the largest category, at 31.5% of all blocks, followed by communication (22.0%), utility (16.8%), avoidance (15.0%), planning (11.6%) and self-care (3.1%). 19 of 25 towers contained at least one avoidance block, and only 7 contained any self-care.

The daily arc

Composition by period resembled the arc described in the qualitative study. Early mornings were led by utility (41.3%), mostly turning off the alarm and checking the time, followed by communication (22.5%) and avoidance (21.3%). Mid-morning and afternoon shifted to planning (43.4%) and communication (39.8%). Evenings were predominantly consumption (77.1%). Night split between avoidance (47.1%) and consumption (33.8%), with utility, mostly setting the alarm, at 17.6%.

Choices relative to the palette

The arc could in principle be an artefact of the vocabulary, since participants can only choose what each palette offers. Comparing the share of each category in the palette with its share of the blocks people chose separates the two. In several cells the choices depart strongly from what was offered. Early-morning utility makes up 17.6% of that palette and 41.3% of the blocks chosen there. Self-care makes up 35.3% of the early-morning palette and 8.8% of the blocks chosen. Evening consumption rises from 58.3% of the palette to 77.1% of choices, and midday communication from 18.2% to 39.8%.

These departures suggest that participants’ own choices shape the arc beyond what each palette makes available. The palette still bounds the arc in two places. Avoidance is unavailable at midday and in the evening, and night offers no self-care or planning, so some cells of Figure 4 are fixed at zero by design.

Sequence features

The longest unbroken run of a single category was 8 consecutive consumption blocks. Three consecutive avoidance blocks at night occur in 3 towers. The night spiral penalty is defined on the depleting effect, and the attribute map marks most night consumption blocks as depleting as well, so the penalty reaches 4 towers (Section 09).

Because all four palettes are on one screen, blocks do not have to be placed in period order. 4 of 25 towers have at least one block placed after a block from a later period, and in 1 of them the first block placed was a mid-morning block. The first-action term and the first-three rules use position in the tower, so in that case the opening action is a midday activity.

09

Pilot scores

The scoring module and the attribute map are part of the source code of Build Your Phone Day. The most recent commit to the code is dated 21 March 2026 at 04:03 UTC, eight minutes before the first pilot submission, so we treat that version as the one that scored the pilot. We reimplemented the scoring module in Python, read all 79 entries of the attribute map directly from the source, and scored each retained tower in the order its blocks were placed. The formulas, weights, penalties and bands in the code match the technical notes term for term, and the reimplementation reproduces the worked example in Section 06.

Overall scores

The mean overall score is 21.4 out of 100 (median 22.3, standard deviation 16.4), and scores range from 0.0 to 58.0. Of the 25 towers, 21 fall in the lowest band, 3 in the second-lowest and 1 in the middle band, and none reaches 65. Two towers were clamped to 0, one of them from −39.25.

On average the period part contributes 20.3 points, the first action 7.6 points, and the penalties subtract 8.1 points. The three parts do not add up to the mean overall score because two towers were clamped at 0. Fourteen of 25 towers received no penalty at all, so the low totals come mainly from the period scores.

Period scores

The low totals come mostly from two of the four period models. Night is scored 0 in 22 of the 23 towers that contain a night block, and early morning in 12 of 25. Mid-morning is the only period with a median above 40. Evening sits in between, with a median of 10.2, because the reels, stories and catch-up blocks that make up most evening towers each carry a small negative value.

Properties of the specification

Rescoring the pilot with the scoring code settles several questions that the documents left open, and it shows properties of the specification that are worth resolving before the score is used in research. Most of them come from the combination of the period palettes and the attribute map, which together decide which weighted terms a tower can reach at all.

The two largest positive terms in the night model, restorative effect and self-care, cannot be earned by any night block. The night palette has no self-care block, and the attribute map marks none of its 17 blocks as restorative. With half of the positive weight out of reach, the best night score any tower can receive is 30 (Table 9), and the night spiral risk tag, which applies below 40, is attached to every tower that contains a night block. Mid-morning has the opposite property, since no mid-morning block is reactive, depleting or avoidance, so three of its penalties never apply and its lowest block value is −30. Each of these terms can be resolved in one of two ways for the next version: add blocks with that category or attribute to the palette, or remove the term and state that the period is defined without it.

Clamping at the period level

Each period score is clamped to the range 0 to 100 before the periods are combined. In the pilot, 22 of 23 night scores and 12 of 25 early-morning scores were negative before clamping and were recorded as 0. The mean night score before clamping was −32.3, so the clamp removes most of the variation between nights. A tower with one doom-scrolling block at night and a tower with six receive the same night score. Whether this is intended should be decided together with the night ceiling, since raising the ceiling without changing the clamp would still leave most nights at 0.

A first-action term with little spread

In the pilot, 20 of 25 towers opened with a utility block and would receive the same first-action contribution of 8.5 points. The remaining five received 1.5 (two towers), 3.5, 4.5 and 9.5. In this sample the term mostly records whether a person turned off an alarm first. Turning off the alarm also counts as utility for the early foundation rule, so a single alarm block both earns the 85 and protects the tower from the 12-point penalty. Whether an alarm should count as a foundation for the day is a design question the next version should answer explicitly.

Order of placement

The scoring rules that use order assume that position in the tower follows the day. The one-screen layout allows otherwise, and 4 of 25 towers include blocks placed out of period order. Two remedies are available: sort blocks by period, keeping their order within each period, before computing the first-action term and the first-three rules, or present the periods one at a time in the interface.

Validation plan

We see five stages between this specification and a measure that others should trust, and we intend to take them in order. The specification is versioned, and any change to weights, attributes or penalties increments the version so that reported findings stay attached to the specification that produced them.

  1. Taxonomy reliability

    Independent raters from HCI, psychology and outside academia annotate all 79 blocks on the four attributes. Agreement is reported with Krippendorff’s alpha, and contested blocks are re-specified, moved to mixed or neutral, or flagged.

  2. Weight sensitivity

    Each weight and period constant is varied across plausible ranges, and the stability of score rankings is measured over real and synthetic towers. The output is a map of which conclusions are robust and which depend on specific coefficients.

  3. Reliability of the instrument

    The same day is reconstructed twice, days apart, to test stability, and reconstructed towers are compared with logged phone use in a consenting subsample to test accuracy.

  4. Construct validity

    The PDS is administered alongside problematic use scales such as the SAS-SV, wellbeing and affect measures, sleep quality and a single-item rating of the day, in a sample large enough to test convergent, discriminant and known-group patterns.

  5. Generalisation of the normative model

    The qualitative grounding is repeated in at least one structurally different population, such as shift workers or non-students, to test whether the period structure and the signs of the weights hold.

Three changes follow directly from this pilot and can be made before Stage 1. The first is to resolve the unreachable terms in Table 13, the night ceiling among them, and the open points in Table 15. The second is to add the missing audiobook entry to the attribute map. The third is to store the score, the specification version and the block attributes with each submission, so that every tower can be rescored under any later version of the specification.

Limitations and future work

  • Hand-set weights. The literature motivates the signs and rough ordering of the weights, and it does not determine their magnitudes. Two defensible readings of the same evidence could produce different tables.
  • Author-assigned attributes. Some assignments are uncontroversial and others, such as whether checking email is reactive, are contestable. Misclassification passes directly into scores.
  • A situated normative model. Mornings for intention, evenings for leisure and nights for recovery comes from students in India and a literature weighted toward student samples. Shift workers, caregivers and people in other contexts live differently shaped days, and the current specification would mis-score them by design.
  • Reconstruction and self-report. Towers are retrospective and open to recall error and self-presentation. The instrument records neither duration nor context, so a two-minute weather check and a ninety-minute scroll each occupy one block.
  • A small, self-selected pilot. 25 usable towers from a public link, most of them submitted on one day, with no demographic information and no way to tell whether two towers came from the same person.
  • Scores recomputed after the fact. The export stores neither scores nor attributes, so we rescored the towers with a port of the scoring code. The last commit to the code predates the first submission by eight minutes, and we cannot confirm that the deployed build was identical to it.

A scored day also carries a risk of its own. It could feed the guilt we documented in the qualitative study, now with a number attached. The specification treats bounded evening consumption as positive, treats unreported periods as unobserved and names what is working before what is not, and whether those choices are enough is an empirical question about how people receive their scores. That question belongs in the validation plan alongside the psychometrics.
